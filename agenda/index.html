01

Research Agenda

Understanding an uncertain world.

Laplace Research is an independent research organisation working to solve forecasting and, as part of that work, to advance the science of temporal intelligence.

Our research explores how intelligent systems can reconstruct the past, infer the present and reason about possible futures. We believe that understanding uncertainty, evidence and causality is fundamental to building intelligence systems that can contribute meaningfully to scientific discovery and human decision-making.

This page describes the scientific questions behind our work, not a list of results. Individual releases answer narrow questions; the agenda is the set of larger problems they are steps towards. It is organised around five connected research directions, and we expect it to change as the evidence does.

02

Temporal intelligence

By temporal intelligence we mean the ability to reason carefully about states of the world that cannot be observed directly: those that have passed, those that are hidden now, and those that have not yet happened. Forecasting is the part of this problem we work on first and most. The other two parts share its structure, because each one is an inference from incomplete evidence to a distribution over possible states.

Past · Retrodiction
Inferring historical events, causal relationships and previously unobserved states from the evidence that survives.
Present · State estimation
Understanding the underlying state of a system from noisy, incomplete or contradictory observations.
Future · Forecasting
Estimating probability distributions over possible outcomes and future states.

Evidence, uncertainty, causal reasoning and belief revision connect the three problems. A model that cannot weigh evidence will misjudge the past as easily as the future. None of this implies certainty about either: a reconstruction of the past is an inference, not a record, and a forecast is a probability, not knowledge of what will happen.

03 · Direction 01

Forecasting

Core question

How can intelligent systems generate reliable, calibrated forecasts about an uncertain and changing world?

Forecasting is the foundational direction of Laplace Research. We study how models can combine evidence, reason probabilistically, update predictions and communicate uncertainty across different domains. A forecast is calibrated when events it gives a 70% chance happen about 70% of the time.

Research topics

  • Probabilistic reasoning
  • Forecast calibration
  • Evidence-grounded prediction
  • Long-horizon forecasting
  • Cross-domain generalisation
  • Dynamic forecast revision
  • Forecasting under distribution shift
  • Superforecasting methods
  • Forecast aggregation
  • Reasoning under incomplete information

Open questions

  1. How can models stay calibrated across unfamiliar domains and time horizons?
  2. Can forecasting ability generalise beyond the historical distributions a model has seen?
  3. How should a system incorporate conflicting or incomplete evidence?
  4. How can forecasts stay reliable when publishing them influences the outcome?
  5. Which representations of uncertainty are most useful for human decisions?

Long-term objective

Forecasting systems whose assessments of possible future events are empirically reliable, transparent and appropriately calibrated.

Related published work

  • Model release29 Aug 2026

    Tarot-Draw

    Three binary forecasting models (1.7B, 4B, 8B) trained on the same 81,870 resolved questions: a controlled study of what model scale changes in calibration and resolution, and what it does not.

04 · Direction 02

Retrodiction and state estimation

Core question

How can intelligent systems reconstruct unobserved states of the world from incomplete evidence?

Retrodiction is inference about the past: working out what probably happened, and why, from the information available now. It is forecasting run backwards, and it carries the same uncertainty. State estimation applies the same reasoning to the present, inferring the current condition of a system that can only be partly observed.

Research topics

  • Historical state reconstruction
  • Bayesian inference
  • Inverse problems
  • Causal inference
  • Latent state estimation
  • Evidence attribution
  • Historical uncertainty
  • Temporal consistency
  • Inference under partial observability

Open questions

  1. How can models distinguish plausible historical reconstructions from verified historical facts?
  2. How should a system represent several histories that are consistent with the same evidence?
  3. Can models reliably infer causal relationships from incomplete observations?
  4. How can a reconstruction communicate uncertainty that more data cannot remove?
  5. How should conflicting historical sources be reconciled?

Long-term objective

Systems that produce evidence-grounded, uncertainty-aware reconstructions of past and present states, and that never present an inference as the historical record.

05 · Direction 03

Epistemic intelligence

Core question

How should intelligent systems form, maintain, challenge and revise their beliefs?

Reliable forecasting depends on more than predicting outcomes. It depends on how a model acquires and evaluates what it knows. By epistemic intelligence we mean the capacities involved: judging the reliability of evidence, holding confidence in proportion to it, changing a belief for good reasons and not for bad ones, and recognising when the available information does not settle a question.

Research topics

  • Belief formation and revision
  • Evidence provenance
  • Epistemic calibration
  • Metacognition
  • Confidence estimation
  • Abstention
  • Resistance to misleading evidence
  • Reasoning under disagreement
  • Intellectual independence
  • Recognising the limits of knowledge

Open questions

  1. When should an intelligent system revise an existing belief?
  2. How can a system tell persuasive information from reliable evidence?
  3. Can models recognise when the information available to them is insufficient?
  4. How can confidence stay aligned with the support the evidence provides?
  5. How can models resist social pressure without becoming unreasonably resistant to new evidence?

Long-term objective

Systems whose beliefs stay responsive to evidence, appropriately uncertain and resistant to manipulation.

Related published work

  • Model release24 Nov 2026

    Signet

    Open models (2B, 4B, 9B) trained to adopt genuine tool records and reject the same record pasted into chat, including in formats never seen in training, while still accepting valid corrections.

  • Benchmark10 Nov 2026

    ConvictionBench

    How language models keep, revise or withhold an answer under evidence of varying quality and social pressure, across 26,478 episodes. Paper (PDF).

  • Models10 Nov 2026

    Conviction Series

    Three Qwen3-1.7B adapters that compare supervised fine-tuning, GRPO and both in sequence as ways to train for belief revision.

06 · Direction 04

World simulation and counterfactual reasoning

Core question

How can intelligent systems model alternative histories, possible futures and the consequences of different assumptions?

A forecast is more informative when a system can also reason about how it would change if the underlying conditions did. This direction studies computational representations of changing worlds, and counterfactuals: statements about what would have happened, or would happen, under conditions other than the actual ones. A simulated outcome is a conditional scenario. It is not a prediction, and it is not evidence about the world.

Research topics

  • World models
  • Counterfactual reasoning
  • Causal simulation
  • Scenario generation
  • Synthetic environments
  • Dynamical systems
  • Multi-agent environments
  • Alternative historical trajectories
  • Long-horizon temporal consistency

Open questions

  1. How can simulated scenarios stay causally coherent?
  2. How can a system tell plausible counterfactuals from unsupported speculation?
  3. Can synthetic environments improve how forecasting generalises to the real world?
  4. How should uncertainty propagate across long simulation horizons?
  5. How can counterfactual reasoning be evaluated when the alternative outcome can never be observed?

Long-term objective

Scientifically grounded systems for exploring possible worlds and understanding how different conditions could change outcomes, with every scenario labelled as conditional.

07 · Direction 05

Forecasting infrastructure

Core question

What scientific and computational infrastructure is needed to build and evaluate reliable temporal intelligence?

Progress in forecasting needs more than better model architectures. It needs trustworthy data, rigorous evaluation and experiments that others can reproduce. A forecasting evaluation is only meaningful if the model could not have seen the answer, so temporal leakage, information from after a question’s cutoff reaching training or evaluation, is a central concern of this direction.

Research topics

  • Temporally indexed datasets
  • Forecasting benchmarks
  • Contamination-resistant evaluation
  • Reinforcement learning environments
  • Synthetic data generation
  • Scoring rules
  • Evaluation infrastructure
  • Reproducible training methods
  • Open-source research tooling
  • Forecast resolution and verification

Open questions

  1. How can evaluations prevent temporal leakage?
  2. How can forecasting benchmarks stay relevant as their events resolve?
  3. How should models be evaluated across very different forecasting domains?
  4. Which training environments encourage reliable probabilistic reasoning?
  5. How can an evaluation tell memorisation apart from genuine predictive ability?

Long-term objective

The research infrastructure needed for reproducible, sustained progress in forecasting: data, benchmarks, environments and tools that others can inspect and build on.

Related published work

    An open benchmark and dataset (CC BY 4.0) with published splits, scoring rules and oracles, and evaluation code.

  • Code · Results24 Nov 2026

08

How we do research

Six principles connect the five directions. They describe how we intend every piece of work to be done, whichever direction it belongs to. How we evaluate, release and govern that work is set out in Safety & Responsibility.

  1. 01

    Empirical grounding

    Research claims should rest on measurable evidence and clearly documented methods.

  2. 02

    Mathematical foundations

    Where it helps, we draw on probability, statistics, information theory, causal inference and optimisation.

  3. 03

    Reproducibility

    We make research artifacts and methods available wherever that is feasible and responsible.

  4. 04

    Temporal integrity

    Training and evaluation should respect what information was available when, and prevent temporal contamination.

  5. 05

    Interdisciplinary research

    Forecasting spans machine learning, mathematics, economics, cognitive science and the study of complex systems.

  6. 06

    Responsible openness

    We weigh the value of open research against its potential for misuse, in proportion to the risk. See model evaluation and release.

09

Research horizons

The directions above can be read as three broad horizons, each building on the one before. They are scientific ambitions, not a product roadmap: they carry no dates, performance targets or promised capabilities, and we may find that some of them cannot be reached.

  1. Horizon I

    Reliable forecasting foundations

    Reliable forecasting models, calibration methods, datasets and evaluations. Our published work so far sits within this horizon.

  2. Horizon II

    Generalised temporal intelligence

    Systems that reason across reconstruction of the past, estimation of the present and forecasting of the future.

  3. Horizon III

    Integrated temporal reasoning

    Whether forecasting, retrodiction, evidence reasoning and world simulation can be brought together in one coherent framework. This is an open question, not a plan.

10

An open scientific pursuit.

This agenda is a set of connected scientific questions, not a fixed destination. As our understanding of forecasting and temporal intelligence develops, we expect these directions to change with new evidence, methods and findings. New releases will be linked from the direction they inform.

We welcome collaboration with researchers and institutions interested in advancing these foundations.
