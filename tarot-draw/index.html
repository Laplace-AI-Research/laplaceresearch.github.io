Tarot-Draw: Three binary forecasting models trained on the same 81,870 questions

Model release August 29, 2026 · Arya Somu, Bruce Nshuti Hirwa · 8 min read

Today we are releasing Tarot-Draw, a family of three binary probability estimators (1.7B, 4B, and 8B) trained on the exact same 81,870 forecasting questions. It is a controlled study of what model scale changes in probabilistic judgment, and what it does not.

Give a Tarot-Draw model a question, its resolution criteria, a scheduled resolution date, and an as_of date, and it returns a single calibrated number between zero and one. There is no retrieval layer, news feed, market price, or generated explanation. Isolating this single capability lets us hold corpus, targets, seed, learning rate, and evaluation footing completely fixed so that parameter count is the only variable.

Our central finding is simple: calibration stayed flat, while resolution did not. All three models learn when to be cautious, achieving near-zero calibration error regardless of size. What the smallest 1.7B model gives up is resolution—the ability to separate easier questions from harder ones. Above 4B, however, gains flatten out: Tarot-Draw 4B and 8B are statistically indistinguishable on the development split.

All three model weights are available in the Hugging Face model collection, and every forecast, split definition, and metric script ships with the open verification repositories.

What Tarot-Draw is

Tarot-Draw is a family of no-retrieval probability estimators for binary questions about future events. The models are intended to provide a calibrated prior when current evidence is sparse, a prediction market does not exist, or a forecaster needs a consistent baseline before incorporating external updates.

This scope is deliberately narrower than a general forecasting agent. Because nothing is fetched at inference time, every number reflects only the model’s parametric prior conditioned on the question framing and horizon.

One variable: model size

The 1.7B, 4B, and 8B models share the same corpus, targets, seed, learning rate, and evaluation footing. Holding those choices fixed turns the family into a controlled experiment. The central question is not simply whether a larger model scores better—it is which component of forecast quality actually moves as capacity increases.

Calibration stayed flat. Resolution did not.

Under the Murphy decomposition of the Brier score, total error splits into uncertainty (a property of the questions), calibration error (how well predicted probabilities match empirical frequencies), and resolution (how sharply predictions separate outcomes that happen from those that do not). Every size learns calibration to within a thousandth of a Brier point. The 1.7B trails the 4B by 0.0119 Brier (paired 95% interval: +0.0080 to +0.0158), and virtually all of that gap comes from lower resolution.

Development split summary

Table 1 reports headline Brier, calibration, and resolution scores on the development split. Question-level intervals do not include run-to-run training variance; differences below roughly 0.008 Brier should be treated as unresolved.

Choosing a model

Start with Tarot-Draw 4B. It matches the 8B model within the statistical resolution of this experiment while requiring half the deployment footprint. The other two sizes remain useful when hardware is the binding constraint or when reproducing the scaling curve directly.

A result you can inspect

The published forward split contains 277 questions that resolved after a freeze date committed in public before it passed. On that forward split, the 4B scored 0.1831 Brier, the 8B scored 0.1893, and the 1.7B scored 0.2030. These are clean point estimates, though the split is too small to support a ranking between the 4B and 8B.

Confidence intervals use 10,000 paired, question-clustered bootstrap samples. Every forecast, split metadata file, metric implementation, and verification script ships with the release, and recomputing every headline number requires no model, GPU, or network access.

Where the prior stops

Tarot-Draw is a starting estimate, not an oracle. Its calibration should be re-measured whenever the venue, horizon distribution, or question type changes. Four structural limits apply to the current release:

  • Current information is absent. The models cannot see breaking news, private evidence, or live market prices. Any forecast that depends on recent developments requires an external update step.
  • Mechanical questions remain difficult. Thresholds, counts, scores, and questions requiring precise arithmetic sit outside the strengths of a parametric judgment prior.
  • Very short horizons are weak. At horizons under seven days, the tested small model does not beat a market crowd at any point in a question’s life.
  • Some domains are underrepresented. Sports and obscure local elections are measured weaknesses in the present release.
