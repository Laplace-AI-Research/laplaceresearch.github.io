Introducing Laplace Oracle: Our family of models that buy evidence before they forecast

Model release November 30, 2026 · By Arya Somu & Bruce Nshuti Hirwa · 11 min read

We trained six internal models to buy observations, report probabilities and decide when to stop. In one controlled diagnostic, every trained model declined a duplicate reading, yet every model changed its forecast when that reading was supplied anyway.

One sensor, two displays

A hidden signal is either on or off, and each is equally likely. A sensor, call it A, watches the signal. When the signal is on, sensor A reads positive 80% of the time. When the signal is off, it reads positive 20% of the time. Display A shows sensor A’s reading. Next to it, display B also shows a reading, but display B is wired to the same sensor. It cannot disagree with display A, because it is the same measurement shown on a second screen.

Suppose display A shows positive. A positive reading is four times as likely when the signal is on (80%) as when it is off (20%), so the odds move from 1:1 to 4:1. The exact probability that the signal is on is 80%. Display B will also show positive, so paying to read it buys nothing, and seeing it anyway should change nothing: the answer stays at 80%. If B were a second, independent sensor with the same accuracy, its positive reading would multiply the odds by four again, to 16:1. That is 16/17, about 94.1%.

Oracle Aquila, one of the six Oracle models, played this world in an ordinary recorded episode. It bought display A, saw a positive reading, reported 80% and stopped without buying display B. That is the exact answer, reached without paying for the copy.

In a separate test, Aquila did not get to choose. We supplied a history in which both displays had already been read and both were positive, with the same wiring description and the same instruction every model receives: a copied reading provides no new evidence. Aquila reported 90.5%. It had declined to pay for the copy, but when the copy was put in front of it, its forecast moved.

Try Oracle and the Second Sensor → Play the same world in the Playground, then compare the recorded episode with the forced-history test.

Those two results are the subject of this article. Deciding which evidence to buy and reporting what the evidence supports are different skills, and a model can show one without the other. The full evaluation also tested whether reinforcement learning helped, and found that a policy which never buys anything beat every trained model on held-out worlds.

Three decisions

Oracle models are trained and evaluated in a controlled setting for evidence acquisition. In every episode the model faces the same three decisions, turn by turn:

  1. Which observation is worth buying? Each sensor reading costs credits from a fixed budget. Some readings are informative, some are redundant and some are overpriced.
  2. What probability does the purchased evidence support? The model reports the probability that a hidden yes-or-no outcome is true, given only what it has bought.
  3. When should investigation stop? Another reading is worth buying only if the expected improvement in the forecast exceeds its price.

These can fail independently. A model can buy the right reading and misread it, or read evidence correctly and keep paying after more evidence stops being worth the price. A single score that blends all three can hide which one went wrong.

Worlds where the answer is known

Every episode takes place in a synthetic binary world: a small probability model with one hidden outcome and a few sensors that can be read. The law of each world is written out in the prompt: the prior, each sensor’s likelihoods, how the sensors depend on one another and what each reading costs. That public description, plus the readings bought so far, is the only input the policy receives. The hidden state, meaning the realized outcome and every reading not yet bought, stays inside the simulator. So do the evaluator’s labels.

Because each law is explicit and small, the exact posterior, the probability that a perfect reasoner would report after any history, can be computed by enumerating every possibility. That lets us score a model’s probability against the correct answer, not only against the outcome that happened to be drawn. It also gives an exact acquisition reference: a symbolic policy that plans its purchases from the known law and reports exact posteriors. It is a reference point, not a trained model.

The motivation is familiar. Several reports that look independent can trace back to one source, and counting them separately overstates confidence. That analogy is why we built a copy-sensor diagnostic. It is not what we tested. Oracle did not evaluate news aggregation, financial forecasting or real-world source tracing.

The experiment

The base model is Thinking Machines’ Inkling 2 Mini. On top of it we trained six rank-16 LoRA adapters: small low-rank weight updates to the attention and unembedding layers that leave the base weights unchanged. Training had two stages.

First, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on 3,315 demonstration turns from an exact teacher. The teacher saw only the public law and the evidence it had bought, computed beliefs exactly and never looked at an unbought reading or the outcome. We ran SFT with three random seeds, 173, 271 and 389, producing Oracle Lyra, Oracle Aquila and Oracle Cygnus. Second, each SFT model continued with reinforcement learning (RL) on the evaluation reward, producing one descendant each: Oracle Vega from Lyra, Oracle Altair from Aquila and Oracle Deneb from Cygnus.

Training worlds came from three families. In independent worlds, sensors err independently given the outcome. In shared-source worlds, two views descend from one noisy instrument, including exact copies and near copies. In common-cause worlds, a hidden regime drives the outcome and several sensors together. Two structural holdouts never appeared in training. In XOR worlds, the outcome is a noisy exclusive-or of two hidden bits, so neither bit’s sensor is useful alone. In switch worlds, a regime decides which sensor is informative, and one of its reports is duplicated.

The reward used for RL and for evaluation is, for a valid final forecast p and outcome y (1 if true, 0 if false):

r=− (p−y)2 −0.01⁢c −0.02t512

r reward p reported probability y outcome c credits spent t generated tokens

The first term is the Brier score, a squared-error measure that is best on average when the reported probability equals the exact posterior. An episode without a valid final forecast scores −2.1, below any valid result. Credits and the token penalty are accounting units for the experiment, not money. Higher reward is better, and every reward in this article is negative.

Every model was evaluated on the same worlds at four evidence budgets: 0, 2, 4 and 8 credits. There were 384 in-distribution (IID) worlds from the training families and 452 structural-holdout worlds, 226 XOR and 226 switch. With seven model arms (the base and six adapters), that is 23,408 model episodes: 10,752 IID and 12,656 structural-holdout. Those are episodes, not independent worlds. Each world appears 28 times, once per arm and budget, so our intervals resample worlds, not episodes. The primary comparison, fixed in advance, is the paired change in mean net reward after RL on the structural holdouts, with a practical threshold of +0.010.

Declining a copy is not ignoring it

We ran the copy-sensor world from the opening on all seven models under two fixed conditions. In the first, the model sees display A’s positive reading and chooses whether to buy display B or stop, with a forecast. In the second, the forced history, both positive readings are already in the transcript and the model only reports a forecast.

Every trained adapter stopped after display A without buying the copy. Every trained adapter also moved its forecast when the copy was supplied, by between 10.5 percentage points (Aquila) and 22.2 points (Lyra). The untrained base model did the reverse: it paid for display B, then held its forecast at 80%. Choosing not to buy a reading and treating that reading as uninformative turned out to be separable behaviors, in both directions.

Movement is not the same as error in both conditions. Only Aquila and Deneb reported the exact 80% after display A. Oracle Altair reported 66.7% after display A and exactly 80% after the copy, so the copy moved it from a wrong answer to the right one. The diagnostic shows that the trained models’ forecasts were not invariant to a reading that carries no information. It does not show that every model overshot.

These are individual recorded responses to one fixed history each, decoded at temperature zero. They are evidence that the pattern exists in these models, not a measure of how often it occurs or of reasoning ability in general.

The RL effect depends on the seed

On the structural holdouts, the RL models averaged +0.01325 more net reward per episode than their SFT parents, just above the +0.010 threshold. The 95% world-and-seed interval runs from −0.00825 to +0.03314 and includes zero. These data leave the direction of the RL effect uncertain.

We report two intervals because they answer different questions. The narrower world-cluster interval, +0.00857 to +0.01800, treats the three seeds as fixed and resamples only worlds. It says how precisely we measured these three particular pairs. The world-and-seed interval resamples seeds as well, so it speaks to what another training run might produce. It is wide because the three pairs disagree: Lyra to Vega gained 0.01648, Aquila to Altair gained 0.03304 and Cygnus to Deneb lost 0.00976.

A policy that never looks scored higher

The prior-only baseline buys nothing. It reports the public prior and stops. On the structural holdouts it scored −0.21697. Every trained adapter scored lower, from Oracle Altair at −0.23034 to Oracle Deneb at −0.26787. The exact acquisition reference scored −0.18166.

The prior-only result is not a fluke of one baseline. Three other symbolic policies, which choose purchases with a simple rule and then report the exact posterior, also beat every adapter: random legal purchases (−0.21100), buying in ascending price order (−0.19237) and greedy one-step value (−0.19292). Nor is the gap an artifact of token accounting. The largest average token penalty among the adapters was under 0.002 per episode, while the gap between prior-only and the best adapter is 0.01337.

Table 3 shows where the gap comes from. Oracle Altair’s Brier score, 0.21675, is almost identical to prior-only’s 0.21697, but Altair spent an average of 1.204 credits per episode to get there. On these holdouts the adapters’ purchases did not improve their forecasts enough to pay for themselves. The all-evidence row, at −0.10064, is a privileged diagnostic: it receives every reading free of charge. It shows how much the evidence could support, and it is not a policy anyone could deploy.

Valid output is not correct reasoning

The clearest change from training was in output validity. On the structural holdouts, the base model produced a valid final forecast in 52.5% of episodes; every adapter did so in 100%. Each invalid episode scores −2.1, so invalid episodes alone account for about −1.0 of the base model’s −1.14332 mean (47.5% × −2.1).

Validity is necessary, but it is a different property from reporting the right probability or buying the right evidence. A well-formed forecast can still be wrong, and a well-formed purchase can still be poor value. The copy diagnostic and the baseline comparison each show one of those gaps in models that never produced an invalid answer on these holdouts.

What we take from it

Test acquisition and reporting separately

A model that declines redundant evidence can still be moved by it. Fixed-history tests, where the model cannot choose its evidence, measured something that purchase decisions alone did not.

Include cheap policies and exact references

Without the prior-only baseline, the adapters’ large gain over the base would read as success. An exact reference and a do-nothing policy put that gain in context.

Report seed uncertainty with world uncertainty

An interval that holds seeds fixed excluded zero here; one that resamples seeds did not. With three seeds, the second is the honest one for claims about the training method.

Limitations

  • The tasks are synthetic binary worlds with explicit, stated probability laws.
  • Episodes are short, and the registry has three training families.
  • Structural generalization was tested on two held-out mechanisms, XOR and switch.
  • Three training seeds give limited evidence about training variability.
  • We do not know what the base model saw in pretraining, including any overlap with these tasks.
  • The copy-sensor results are fixed diagnostic examples, not a broad reasoning benchmark.
  • We have not shown any improvement in real-world forecasting.
  • Running the base model with an adapter outside the training provider has not been shown to match the evaluated outputs.

Methods

Base model
Thinking Machines Inkling 2 Mini, used under its upstream terms. See Licenses.
Adapters
Rank-16 LoRA on attention and unembedding layers; MLP frozen.
SFT
3,315 exact-teacher turns (76,476 supervised tokens); loss on assistant output only. Learning rate 0.0002; checkpoint chosen on 256 development worlds.
RL
32 batches of 16 worlds, four trajectories per world, learning rate 0.00003, sampling temperature 1.
Evaluation
Temperature 0; at most 128 generated tokens per turn and 512 per episode. Budgets 0, 2, 4, 8 credits. Same worlds for every arm.
Data
2,000 SFT worlds and 512 RL worlds from the three training families. 384 IID and 452 structural-holdout evaluation worlds.
Intervals
Paired bootstrap, 4,000 draws, families weighted equally. World-cluster intervals resample worlds; world-and-seed intervals also resample seed pairs. Both condition on the family registry.
Invalid output
Malformed output, illegal queries or no valid stop: reward −2.1.
