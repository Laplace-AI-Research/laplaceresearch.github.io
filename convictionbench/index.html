ConvictionBench

Evidence-sensitive belief revision in language models

When challenged, a language model has to decide whether to keep its answer, revise it, or say the evidence is insufficient. ConvictionBench tests how that decision changes with the evidence and with the pressure to agree.

By Arya Somu and Bruce Nshuti Hirwa

02

Evidence and social pressure

ConvictionBench v0.1 contains 26,478 generated episodes across arithmetic, ledgers, logic, and small rule-based worlds. A deterministic solver determines the expected answer and decision.

Matched episodes vary the wording of a challenge, the quality of its evidence, and the channel the evidence arrives through. The structural split holds out whole problem types. A separate natural-history evaluation challenges the model’s own first answer.

In a control using identical record text, Anchor adopted the record 62% of the time from a tool message and 58% of the time from a plain user message, compared with 8% when the user added the forger’s wording. That result shows how much the measured resistance depended on the framing.

The tasks use short synthetic dialogues and a fixed response format. The results describe behavior under those conditions and give limited evidence about real-world authentication or manipulation.

03

Laplace Conviction Series

Three experimental Qwen3-1.7B LoRA adapters test different ways to train for belief revision.

Anchor often kept its own wrong answers after valid corrections in natural history. Vector had the highest held-out final accuracy of the three in the original setup, with weaker forged-record resistance than the base model on the same split. Its accuracy advantage over Anchor was sensitive to the seed and prompt. Meridian resisted more forged records than the other adapters on held-out types and had lower final accuracy than Vector.
